Digital crime scene slides 4–6

A digital crime scene is the virtual or electronic environment where a cybercrime or digital incident happened. Working on it means collecting, analysing and preserving digital evidence to understand the crime.

7 key characteristics

  1. Virtual nature – it exists in the virtual world (computers, networks, digital devices) where electronic evidence is stored. Not like a normal physical crime scene.
  2. Data and artifacts – contains files, logs, system configurations, network traffic and other digital traces left by criminals.
  3. Dynamic environment – digital data changes fast, so investigators must work quickly to save evidence before it is changed or damaged.
  4. Specialized tools – needs special forensic tools to collect, analyse and preserve evidence in a forensically sound way.
  5. Remote investigations – may involve remote systems; investigators may need to work remotely or with people in other countries/places.
  6. Multidisciplinary approach – mixes computer science, cybersecurity, law enforcement and legal procedures.
  7. Legal considerations – evidence must follow legal standards so it is admissible in court. Chain of custody, proper documentation and best practices are very important.

Victim slides 7–8

A victim is an entity or individual (person or organization) that suffered harm, damage or compromise because of a cybercrime or digital incident.

Key points about victims

  1. Individuals or organizations – e.g. people whose personal information was stolen or misused.
  2. Harm or compromise – many forms: unauthorized access to sensitive data, financial losses, disruption of services, reputation damage.
  3. Forensic investigation – investigators find the details of the crime and its impact on the victim.
  4. Legal considerations – victims play a big role in court cases; collected evidence is used to build a case against the perpetrators (attackers).

3 principles of digital forensics slide 16

  1. Securing the crime scene – block any access to the suspected evidence, document all processes and connections, disconnect wireless connections, etc.
  2. Limiting evidence interaction – touch the evidence as little as possible, e.g. capture the RAM first to avoid damage.
  3. Maintaining chain of custody – a record of the order in which evidence was collected, date and timestamps, and which investigator accessed and handled it.

8-step digital forensic process slides 17–18

  1. Identification – first step at the scene: know the purpose of the investigation and spot possible evidence.
  2. Preservation – make sure evidence is not tampered (changed).
  3. Collection – acquire evidence properly without harming it, and pack it in a Faraday bag (blocks wireless signals).
  4. Examination – careful inspection before analysis; comes before analysis (a precursor).
  5. Analysis – the most crucial work: joining pieces of evidence together, retrieving deleted files, etc.
  6. Interpretation – making conclusions from the findings after reconstructing the crime scene.
  7. Documentation – writing a detailed report on the whole investigation.
  8. Presentation – only needed when asked for cross-examination (court); explained in very simple words for normal people.
Order: Identification → Preservation → Collection → Examination → Analysis → Interpretation → Documentation → Presentation (“I Prefer Coffee Every Afternoon In December, Please”).

Windows file systems

  • FAT (File Allocation Table) – older, simple, very compatible, but has limits on file size and partition size.
  • exFAT (Extended FAT) – made for flash storage; supports bigger files than FAT32 without NTFS overhead; good for USB drives and SD cards.
  • NTFS (New Technology File System) – standard Windows file system; large volumes, file permissions, encryption; stronger than FAT/exFAT.
  • ReFS (Resilient File System) – for data centres and enterprise; focuses on data resilience, high availability, scalability.

Get-Acl (PowerShell)

Get-Acl is a PowerShell command that gets the Access Control List (ACL) of a file, folder or registry key. ACLs show who can access an object and what permissions they have (Read, Write, Execute). Open PowerShell from RUN.

PS C:\Users\fasee.ullah\Desktop> get-acl '.\MBOT - ... - Payment.pdf'
Path: MBOT ... Payment.pdf   Owner: CORP\fasee.ullah
Access: NT AUTHORITY\SYSTEM Allow FullControl ...

The output shows the Path, the Owner and the Access rights.

Computer forensics – 3 definitions

  1. Dr. H.B. Wolfe: a methodical series of techniques and procedures for gathering evidence from computers and storage media, presented in court in a clear, meaningful format.
  2. CSI: preservation, identification, extraction, interpretation and documentation of computer evidence, including rules of evidence, legal processes, integrity of evidence, factual reporting, and giving expert opinion in court.
  3. Forensic computing: the science of capturing, processing and investigating data from computers using a method so the evidence is acceptable in a Court of Law.

4 aspects of organizational security

  1. IT security – application, computing, data, information, network security
  2. Physical security – facilities, human, border, biometric security
  3. Financial security – fraud, phishing attacks, botnets, threats from cyber criminals, credit card fraud
  4. Legal security – national security, public security, defamation, copyright information, sexual harassment

Need for computer forensics

  1. Ensure the overall integrity and continued existence of the organization’s computer systems and network
  2. Extract, process and interpret factual evidence to prove the attacker’s actions in court
  3. Efficiently track down perpetrators from different parts of the world
  4. Protect the organization’s money and valuable time

Benefits of forensics readiness

  1. Evidence can be gathered for the company’s defence if sued
  2. Fast, efficient investigation after a major incident with minimal disruption to business
  3. Extends information security to wider threats (IP protection, fraud, extortion)
  4. Structured evidence storage reduces cost and time of internal investigations
  5. Improves and simplifies the law enforcement interface
  6. Allows a proper, in-depth investigation for major incidents

Forensics readiness planning

  1. Define the business states that need digital evidence
  2. Identify the potential evidence available
  3. Determine the evidence collection requirement
  4. Decide the procedure to securely collect evidence in a forensically sound way
  5. Establish a policy to securely handle and store evidence
  6. Make sure monitoring aims to detect and prevent important incidents
  7. Make sure investigative staff are capable of handling and preserving evidence
  8. Document all activities and their impact
  9. Ensure authorized review to support action on the incident

Types of computer crimes

Identity theft, hacking, computer viruses, cyber stalking, drug trafficking, phishing/spoofing, wrongful programming, credit card fraud, online auction fraud, email bombing & SPAM, theft of intellectual property, denial of service attack, debt elimination, web jacking, internet extortion, investment fraud, escrow services fraud, cyber defamation, software piracy, counterfeit cashier’s check, embezzlement.

Role of a forensics investigator

  1. Protects the victim’s computer from damage and viruses
  2. Determines the extent of damage
  3. Gathers evidence in a forensically sound manner
  4. Analyses the evidence and protects it from damage
  5. Prepares the analysis report
  6. Presents acceptable evidence in court

Why you should report cybercrime

  1. Companies may not want to share info because of the impact on their business and the sensitivity of the data
  2. Only by sharing with law enforcement and industry groups can criminals be identified and prosecuted
  3. New threats are identified, and attacks on critical infrastructure and the economy are prevented
  4. Law enforcement’s ability to identify coordinated threats depends on the volume of reporting

Windows forensics slides 3–4

Microsoft Windows forensics = analysing and extracting digital evidence from a Windows computer. It means examining and recovering data, files and other digital artifacts from Windows devices to find the causes of incidents or crimes.

Why important: it is used in criminal investigations, cyber security incidents and incident response. Investigators can find clues, identify perpetrators, and give evidence for legal proceedings.

How to capture memory (full memory dump)

  • FTK Imager 4.5: File menu → Capture Memory…
  • Windows setting: (1) Control Panel → System → Advanced system settings, (2) Startup and Recovery → Settings, (3) under “Write debugging information” choose Complete memory dump. Dump file: %SystemRoot%\MEMORY.DMP.
  • This dump is normally created only after a kernel crash / BSOD (blue screen). After crash and reboot check C:\Windows\MEMORY.DMP and C:\Windows\Minidump.

Data organization in Windows slides 12–17

Five parts: Cluster, Partition, Master Boot Record (MBR), FAT32, NTFS.

  • Cluster – a group of sectors. Typically up to 32KB. Clusters are a logical representation of sectors. (Disk structure: tracks, sectors, clusters.)
  • Partition – a logical division of physical storage so the OS can use each part as a separate unit. Smart users may hide data in hidden or temporarily deleted partitions.
  • MBR disk – MBR holds the boot code and partition table. Supports up to 4 primary partitions; one can be an extended partition that holds many logical drives (e.g. D: NTFS, E: FAT32, F: exFAT). Example: Partition 1 primary DOS/Windows (NTFS), Partition 2 non-DOS (macOS APFS/HFS+), Partition 3 extended DOS, Partition 4 non-DOS (Linux ext4).
  • Master Boot Record (MBR) – every OS starts by reading a boot record at the first location of the disk. The partition table entry includes: boot indicator, starting head/sector/cylinder, system ID, ending head/sector/cylinder, relative sectors, total sectors. MBR ends with the signature 55 AA. Disk layout: MBR (bootloader + partition table) → boot sector → partitions.
  • FAT32 – FAT = File Allocation Table; a structure that keeps vital metadata of files. FAT uses fixed (predefined) ways to store files, which forensic tools use to rebuild (reconstruct) files.
  • NTFS – newer Microsoft standard, more advanced allocation table structure, keeps data compatible for other OSs. Currently used with Windows.

Identifying digital evidence slides 18–19

  • Digital evidence = any information stored or transmitted in digital form
  • U.S. courts accept digital evidence as physical evidence – digital data is treated as a tangible object
  • Groups such as SWGDE (Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence) set standards for recovering, preserving and examining digital evidence

General tasks of investigators

  1. Identify digital information or artifacts that can be evidence
  2. Collect, preserve and document evidence
  3. Analyse, identify and organize evidence
  4. Rebuild evidence or repeat a situation to check the results can be reproduced reliably

Collecting devices and processing a scene must be done systematically.

Rules of evidence slides 20–23

  • Consistent practices help verify your work and increase your credibility.
  • Follow your state’s rules of evidence or the Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE).
  • Evidence admitted in a criminal case can be used in a civil suit, and the other way round.
  • Keep up to date with the latest rulings on collecting, processing, storing and admitting digital evidence.
  • Digital evidence is changed more easily than other physical evidence. The only way to detect changes is to compare the original data with a duplicate.
  • Most federal courts treat computer records as hearsay = secondhand or indirect evidence.
  • Digital records are usually admissible if they qualify as a business record.
  • Computer records are divided into: computer-generated records and computer-stored records.
  • Records must be shown to be authentic and trustworthy to be admitted.
  • Computer-generated records are authentic if the program that made the output works correctly. They are usually an exception to the hearsay rule.
  • Following proper evidence control steps helps make sure the evidence is authentic.

Private-sector incident scenes slides 24–26

  • Private-sector organizations = businesses and government agencies not involved in law enforcement.
  • NGOs must follow state public disclosure laws and the federal Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), making some documents public records. FOIA lets citizens request copies of public documents made by federal agencies.
  • Special category: ISPs and communication companies. ISPs can investigate abuse by their employees, but not customers – except activities that create an emergency.
  • Corporate incident scenes are much easier to investigate and control than criminal scenes; the scene is often a workplace.
  • Companies should show a warning banner and publish a policy saying they can inspect computing assets at will.
  • Corporate investigators should know when they can examine an employee’s computer; every organization needs a well-defined process for starting an investigation.

Law enforcement crime scenes & warrants slides 27–29

  • Know the criminal rules of search and seizure and how a search warrant works.
  • Police may search for and seize evidence only with probable cause – the standard for whether an officer can arrest, search a person/property, or get a warrant.

Terms used in warrants

  • Innocent information – unrelated information often mixed with the evidence. Judges often add a limiting phrase to the warrant so police can separate innocent information from evidence.
  • Plain view doctrine – objects in plain view of an officer who has a right to be there can be seized without a warrant and used as evidence. Three criteria:
    1. The officer is where he/she has a legal right to be
    2. Ordinary senses are not enhanced by advanced technology
    3. The discovery is by chance

Preparing for an evidence search slides 30–39

  1. Identify the nature of the case – including whether it is private or public sector. The nature of the case decides how you proceed and what resources you need.
  2. Identify the type of OS or digital device – hard for law enforcement because the scene isn’t controlled. If you can, estimate the drive size, how many devices to process, and which OSs and hardware are involved.
  3. Determine whether you can seize computers and devices – law enforcement needs a warrant to remove computers to a lab. If removing them would irreparably harm a business, they should not be taken offsite.
  4. Get a detailed description of the location – find potential hazards, work with your HAZMAT team. HAZMAT guidelines: put the target drive in a special HAZMAT bag, technician decontaminates the bag, check for high temperatures.
  5. Use additional technical expertise – specialists in OSs, RAID servers (redundant array of independent disks), databases. Finding the right person can be hard.
  6. Determine the tools you need – initial-response field kit (lightweight, easy to carry) and extensive-response field kit (all tools you can afford). At the scene, take out only what you need.
  7. Prepare the investigation team – review facts, plans and objectives first. Goal of scene processing: collect and secure digital evidence. Digital evidence is volatile; a slow response can lose it.

Securing the scene & acquiring evidence slides 40–43

Securing a computer incident or crime scene – goals

  • Preserve the evidence
  • Keep information confidential
  • Define a secure perimeter – use yellow barrier tape
  • Legal authority for a corporate incident includes trespassing violations

AFIS (Automated Fingerprint Identification System) – a computer system that identifies fingerprints, connected to a central database; used to identify suspects and check thousands of fingerprints at high speed.

Preparing to acquire digital evidence – questions to ask your supervisor

  1. Do you need to take the entire computer and all peripherals and media nearby?
  2. How will you protect the computer and media while transporting them to the lab?
  3. Is the computer powered on when you arrive?
  4. Is the suspect near the computer?
  5. Could the suspect have damaged or destroyed the computer, peripherals or media?
  6. Will you have to separate the suspect from the computer?

Technical advisor – responsibilities

  • Know all aspects of the seized system
  • Direct the investigator handling sensitive material
  • Help secure the scene
  • Help document the planning strategy
  • Conduct ad hoc trainings
  • Document activities
  • Help conduct the search and seizure

Summary slides 44–45

  • Windows forensics = examining and analysing evidence from Windows: acquiring, preserving, examining and interpreting data from hard drives, memory dumps and network traffic.
  • Digital evidence is anything stored or transmitted on electronic or optical media.
  • In the private sector, the scene is often a contained and controlled area.
  • Companies should publish a right-to-inspect policy.
  • Private and public sectors follow the same computing investigation rules.
  • Guard against physically destroying or contaminating evidence.
